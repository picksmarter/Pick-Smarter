Why the Most Likely Winner Isn't Always the Best Pool Pick

It feels obvious: pick the team more likely to win. And if you were only trying to maximize the number of games you get right, that instinct would be correct. But most Pick'em pools don't reward correct picks in isolation — they rank you against everyone else in the pool. That single difference changes the optimal strategy.

Two different games

Predicting winners and winning a pool are related but distinct problems. Predicting winners is about accuracy. Winning a pool is about differentiation with accuracy — being right in ways that not everyone else was also right about. A pick that 95% of the pool also made barely moves your position relative to the field, even when it hits.

A simple way to see it

Picture two teams in the same game. Team A is a 70% favorite that 95% of the pool is picking. Team B is a 55% underdog that only 25% of the pool is picking. Team A is more likely to win outright. But if Team B wins, the 25% of the pool who picked it just separated themselves from the other 75% — including anyone who took the "safe" favorite. Team A being more likely to win doesn't automatically make it the better pool decision; it depends on what picking it actually buys you relative to the field.

This is exactly what Leverage measures

This is the same idea behind Leverage: the gap between a team's real win probability and how often the public is backing it. A team can be the more likely winner and still be a weaker pool decision than a live underdog the field is sleeping on — and Pick Smarter's recommendation weighs both sides of that trade-off rather than defaulting to "just take the favorite."

This doesn't mean always take the underdog either

Chasing differentiation for its own sake is its own mistake — see Why Being Contrarian Isn't Automatically Smart. The goal isn't to be different. It's to be different when the numbers say it's worth it.
